About this demo

VitalOrbit watches heart rate, blood oxygen, sleep, stress, exercise and radiation dose for four fictional astronauts, plus the cabin air around them, and explains every warning in plain language. Value tags say where each number comes from: SIMULATED is a generated demo value, DERIVED is computed from simulated inputs by a stated rule, ESTIMATE is extrapolated from a simulated rate and is not a forecast. VitalOrbit is not a medical device, does not diagnose, and is not endorsed by NASA. The full basis for every number is in Methodology.

    Two Dose Quantities, Kept Apart

    The dose rates in this app (environment planning values and the MSL/RAD and Chang'E-4 measurements behind them) are dose equivalent, in mSv. NASA's career limit of 600 mSv in NASA-STD-3001 is an effective dose. The two quantities are not interchangeable, so VitalOrbit never divides one by the other and shows no time-to-career-limit estimate; the radiation tile says why, next to the compatible daily estimate.

    The daily action level is a demo threshold set at 90% of the environment's nominal daily rate, so the crew is warned before a full day's planned dose accumulates. It is a simulation rule, not a NASA limit.

    Environment sources: ISS 0.4 mSv/day is a planning value inside NASA's published 0.2–0.5 range; the Moon rate is the Chang'E-4 LND surface measurement (Zhang et al., 2020); the Mars surface rate is the MSL/RAD surface measurement at Gale Crater (Hassler et al., 2014). The higher MSL/RAD figure from Zeitlin et al. (2013) was measured during the cruise to Mars and is used nowhere as a surface value. The Spacecraft (transit) rate of 0.6 mSv/day is a simulation assumption, labelled as such in Methodology.

    Mission Clock

    One clock drives the timeline, the exercise sessions and the charts.

    Mission time is a single continuous count of seconds. The Sol number and the elapsed-days figure are derived from that count, so the day rolls over at midnight instead of being fixed at the boot values. One Earth-referenced 24-hour day is used in every environment, including the Mars surface, so the clock, the schedule and the charts always agree. A true Mars sol lasts 24 h 39 min; that distinction is documented here rather than modelled, because mixing two day lengths would desynchronise the schedule.

      Why This Matters

      On a three-year Mars mission the nearest hospital is about 140 million miles away. The astronaut is the first responder, so they need to see their own health clearly, in colour, at a glance, with a plain-language action for every warning.

      NASA’s Human Research Program groups the risks of long missions into five hazards: space radiation, isolation and confinement, distance from Earth, gravity fields, and hostile/closed environments. Their effects include cardiovascular changes, muscle atrophy, bone loss, mental-health effects, and vision and sleep problems. VitalOrbit maps each hazard to the vitals it affects, so a drift is visible days before it becomes an emergency.

      Data & resources used

        Reference ranges used: resting heart rate 60–100 bpm; SpO2 95–100%; sleep target 7–9 h; ISS crew effective dose ~0.2–0.5 mSv/day; NASA career radiation limit 600 mSv effective dose (NASA-STD-3001, 2022). Simulated dose figures in this app are dose equivalent, a different quantity from the effective-dose career limit, so no time-to-limit projection is computed.

        Methodology & parameter basis

        Every number in this app is one of three kinds: a published reference range, a NASA requirement or operational practice, or a simulation assumption used only for this demo. The kinds are not interchangeable, and demo thresholds are never presented as NASA limits. Earth clinical reference ranges (heart rate, SpO2, sleep) describe healthy adults on Earth; spaceflight operational values (dose rates, exercise practice, the career limit) come from flight measurement and NASA standards, and the two kinds are labelled separately below.
